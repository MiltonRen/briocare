BrioCare · Product Requirements Document

The second chair, staffed by software

BrioCare puts an AI co-facilitator — working name Brio — in the second chair of clinician-supervised pediatric group teletherapy. It keeps the turn ledger, draws in the quiet child, and drafts the per-child record, so one therapist can run a group that clinical practice says takes two.

v0.8 · 29 August 2026 Author · Milton Ren Status · build-challenge submission Companions · TDD + field research (Inside the Therapy Circle)

01 · The people

One therapist, three people in the room

Three people meet this product: the clinician who runs the session, the child who lives inside it, and the parent who chose it and pays for it. We design for the clinician — she holds the license and the liability, and she alone decides whether an AI belongs in her room. But the other two set hard requirements of their own, and the product fails if it wins only her.

The group clinician

License
LCSW / LMFT / child psychologist
Setting
Telehealth, from clinic or home office
Group
6–8 children, ages 8–12, closed cohort
Curriculum
Manualized social-skills / anxiety CBT (PEERS-style)

Staffing
Solo — reimbursement doesn't cover a second facilitator
Paperwork
20–30 min per session, individualized per child, usually after hours

Her session is a juggling act the literature has already named: co-facilitated groups split into a dynamic-administrator (runs the room — norms, safety, the exercise) and an analyst-interpreter (watches the room — who's gone quiet, where tension is building, who said something worth linking). On telehealth she performs both jobs at once, through a grid of thumbnails, while also working the mute buttons.

Her three jobs, in her words: run the protocol so every child leaves having participated; keep the room safe while the exercise is live; produce the compliant record without donating my evening.

The target patient · who it must work for

The child: 8–12, and groups are hard for them

Often referred precisely because peer settings are hard. This is the band where the evidence-based programs cluster (Secret Agent Society 8–12, UP-C 8–11, FRIENDS 7–12), and the peer room is the treatment mechanism, not a discount format. BrioCare's clinical scope is ages 6–12; the prototype is tuned to 8–12.

What the child needs from v0: predictable rituals, a warm voice that never puts them on the spot, and ways to participate before they are ready to speak — send a reaction — a feeling, a thumbs-up — answer a selection, or pass. Their whole engagement layer is designed, in section 05.

The parent · who decides it continues

The parent: waited three months, watching closely

They waited months for a seat, are price-sensitive, and judge fast — a bad early experience is the documented dropout driver, and their #1 reported pain is whether their child gets an equal chance to participate. The manuals also cast them as the home co-coach; the roadmap's parent loop serves that role.

What the parent needs from v0: plain consent language about what Brio is and will never do — then evidence: the participation their child actually got, and progress they can see. In the service model the parent chooses BrioCare, and the parent can leave it.

When their needs collide, v0 resolves in this order: the clinician is the adoption gate, the child's experience is the outcome, the parent buys the evidence of both.

At twelve children, uneven participation is not a facilitation failure — it's arithmetic. One child talking for four minutes spends three other children's entire session. AI Fund's own parent survey ranks "every child gets an equal chance to participate" as pain point #1, and a bad early experience is what drives dropout from a closed cohort — where one empty seat also weakens the peer mechanism for everyone who stays.

The sharpest evidence that the room is the bottleneck: the one documented clinic that moved an established children's group program online gave up on the group format entirely, retreating to individual triads. Their stated barriers — can't read subtle emotional shifts on screen, lost peer dynamics — are this product's spec, line for line.

The documentation pain compounds the throughput pain: a group note legally requires an individualized narrative per child, so growing a group from 8 to 12 raises the after-hours burden by half. The growth lever that makes the business work makes her evening worse — unless the record writes itself from evidence captured live.

03 · The wedge

Why a co-facilitator, and why now

It staffs an existing role

The second chair is standard clinical practice

Two-leader groups are the norm in the manuals — Secret Agent Society specifies two facilitators. Brio takes the analyst-interpreter chair: observe, count, remember, surface. That's the half of the job bounded by attention and memory rather than judgment — precisely what software does better than a tired human. We introduce no new clinical behavior, so a licensed clinician can adopt it inside her existing practice.

It stays inside the license

Facilitation, not treatment

Brio never diagnoses, interprets, or makes a therapeutic decision — it manages turns, pacing, prompts, and records, under live clinician supervision. That keeps BrioCare out of "AI therapy" and out of a multi-year device-approval path, while sessions remain billable group psychotherapy (CPT 90853) delivered by the supervising clinician. Go-to-market is measured in weeks, not FDA cycles.

It unlocks the economics

Equity is the constraint on group size

What breaks first as a group grows isn't logistics — it's even participation, the #1 quality signal parents watch. Hold equity mechanically and 6–8 children can become 10–12 (the billing cap), roughly 1.5–2× families per clinician-hour: shorter waitlists, lower price, better clinician pay. The roadmap goes further — breakout rooms create parallel airtime, the only way per-child talk time survives scale, and an AI second chair can sit in every breakout at once. A human co-facilitator never could.

It's timed right

The gap is open and named

Low-latency duplex voice AI crossed the usable threshold this year. The telehealth-group failure mode is documented, recent, and specific. And the supply gap keeps widening: ~5.4 clinical child psychologists per 100,000 children, with 40% of the U.S. living in a designated shortage area. Nobody else is building the second chair — incumbents sell scheduling and notes for individual therapy.

The scaling mechanism

Serial airtime divides. Parallel airtime multiplies.

Shared floor in a 45-minute session≈16:00
One floor, whole group, n = 121:20 / child
Three Brio-held rooms of four, same clock4:00 / child
Therapist roves; every room logged, flagged, visible×3

Breakout rooms are the load-bearing scaling move, not a nice-to-have. Splitting into small rooms is already a documented drawing-out technique — and parallel floors are the only way per-child speaking time survives a bigger group. A human co-facilitator can hold one extra room; Brio holds every room at once — running the exercise structure, capturing acts, escalating anything that needs the clinician, who roves with full sight of all of it.

This lands in Phase 2, deliberately after supervised whole-group pilots earn clinician trust — but v0's architecture treats a session as rooms from day one, so breakouts are an extension, not a rebuild.

The wedge points at a service, not a SaaS. BrioCare's model is services-as-software: employ therapists and deliver pediatric group care end-to-end, with Brio as the operating system of our own clinical practice. The co-facilitator is the first capability that practice needs — and every pilot session it runs is billable revenue, not a free trial for someone else's clinic.

The heads-up loop

Brio asks the room's owner first

Brio never surprises the therapist. Every planned line surfaces on the panel as an intent card — the exact sentence it wants to say, to whom, and why ("Draw out Maya · quietest 8 min"). The autonomy dial has two positions: autonomous, the default, where a line auto-executes after a short veto window unless cancelled; and suggest-only, one tap away, where nothing is voiced until the therapist taps it — where a cautious clinician or an early pilot can choose to live. Cards whose moment passes cancel themselves. Captions and the live equity display always stream; they are displays, not actions. Brio enters the room the way therapy puppets always have — introduced by the therapist in her own words ("she helps us take turns, and she makes mistakes sometimes"), summonable on cue — but with her own eyes on the room.

Control over Brio

  • Intent cards — read the exact sentence, approve or cancel it, before the room hears it
  • Mute Brio — silences every action and drops pending cards; sensing continues, so captions, flags and the ledger keep flowing
  • Autonomy dial — autonomous with a veto window (default) ↔ suggest-only

Control through Brio

  • Cue Brio — one tap summons a contribution: Brio speaks its most useful observation, now
  • Named moves as buttons — Draw out, Block, Cut off, Link, aimed at a child, delivered in Brio's warm voice
  • Participation weight dial — per child, adjustable live: lower it for the kid having a hard week; zero means "no prompts for Leo today"

The child's side of the screen

Play, kept small and in service of participation

The engagement layer is deliberately modest and clinically derived — token economies and gamified programs like Secret Agent Society are already in the evidence base. In v0 the child's playful surface is the reaction-and-selection layer plus Brio's own warmth; the pet economy follows once the participation loop is proven. One governing rule: mechanics point at regulation and participation, never at competition between children. No leaderboards; each child plays only against their own baseline.

Session pet + shared points

Roadmap

Each child's pet gains energy from participation acts — any act, not just speech — and grows sleepy when one voice dominates. Group points tally as the exercise wraps, earned together, never ranked. Deliberately deferred: v0 proves the participation loop first, then the pet economy layers on.

Cohort progression + missions

Roadmap

A pet chosen in week one and decorated across the 12–16-week cohort — a reason to want next week, aimed at the dropout curve — and Secret Agent Society-style missions between sessions, feeding the next review block.

06 · The boundary

The line Brio never crosses

The safety model is a product surface, not fine print — it's what a clinician evaluates before anything else, and it's enforced in architecture rather than in a prompt. The engine chooses from a closed set of facilitation intents; the language model only phrases the chosen intent for an eight-year-old ear. What isn't in the set cannot be said. Mechanism in the TDD.

Brio never

  • Diagnoses, assesses, or labels a child — no clinical language about any child, ever
  • Gives therapeutic interpretation or advice
  • Acts while muted, or against a therapist instruction
  • Speaks to a child outside the therapist-visible, logged channel
  • Controls a child's microphone — it may suggest a mute; muting stays a therapist-only power
  • Makes membership, escalation, or safety-response decisions — it flags, the clinician decides

Brio always

  • Shows its words before speaking them — every line previews on the panel, and in suggest-only mode nothing is voiced without a tap
  • Yields instantly — therapist mute or cancel pre-empts any pending line
  • Speaks only from the closed facilitation-intent set, in warm, low-pressure language
  • Logs every utterance and every intervention decision for clinician review
  • Degrades to silence — when uncertain (garbled audio, ambiguous state), it does nothing and lets the human lead
  • Stays visible — introduced by name by the therapist, and children and parents are told what Brio is and what it will never do

The distress path, concretely. A conservative watchlist runs over every transcript line. On a hit, nothing changes in the children's rooms: Brio stays silent, the panel raises a private, unmissable flag pinned to the exact words and moment, and the therapist decides what happens next. v0's detector is deliberately simple and errs toward flagging; its limits are documented, and tuning it — vocabulary, thresholds, the escalation protocol behind it — is week-one work with the clinical partner.

07 · Evidence

What shaped the build — and what's still unvalidated

Direct user testing with children inside a 48-hour window would be neither possible nor ethical, so v0 is shaped by credible proxy evidence: published treatment manuals (PEERS, Secret Agent Society, Incredible Years), the one peer-reviewed account of moving a children's group program to telehealth, group-note and billing documentation, and AI Fund's own parent survey and info-session findings. Three build decisions trace straight to it: acts-not-seconds (from the drawing-out and token-economy literature), the pressure-ladder draw-out (drawing out is a documented facilitation technique — we automate its timing and the evidence behind it), and notes-from-evidence (the group note's per-child compliance shape).

Explicitly unvalidated

  • Whether children accept and respond to an AI voice in a live group
  • Whether clinicians trust the equity display enough to change their facilitation
  • Whether act-based reinforcement transfers to genuine engagement, not gaming
  • Child speech recognition reliability in real home acoustics
  • State-by-state telehealth, consent, and minors' data rules; payer mechanics

Week-one discovery plan

  1. Co-design sessions with the therapist-in-residence: walk the panel against a real session plan, rename anything that isn't her language
  2. Replay 2–3 recorded group sessions; hand-score participation and compare the engine's ledger against clinician judgment
  3. Run one full mock cohort — clinician at the panel, adults role-playing the children — and let it change the intervention thresholds
  4. Five parent interviews on AI-in-the-room consent language and the pet mechanic
  5. Legal review: California telehealth for minors, session recording, and data handling

08 · The path

From pilot partnerships to an AI-native provider

Each phase has an exit test. The through-line: prove the second chair with partner clinicians, then become the practice that employs them.

Phase 0 · Clinical co-design

Residency wks 1–4

Therapist-in-residence shapes the protocol fit; mock cohorts harden the safety envelope and intervention thresholds; discovery plan above executes.

ExitA licensed clinician agrees to run a real group on it.

Phase 1 · Supervised pilots

Residency wks 5–12

Two to three partner therapists run real closed cohorts under their own licenses in California, with Brio strictly in the second chair. Sessions are billable — revenue inside the 12 weeks. We measure airtime equity against hand-scored baseline, note-minutes returned, cohort completion, and whether the clinician re-books.

ExitEquity and retention beat the solo baseline; clinicians say the load dropped and choose to continue.

Phase 2 · The practice

Post-funding

BrioCare employs therapists and delivers care end-to-end — intake, cohort, documentation — direct to families and through schools. Breakout rooms land here: parallel airtime lets groups reach 10–12 children with per-child talk time held, the scale case a human second chair can't match. Parent loop and booster sessions extend the relationship.

ExitFull cohorts run by BrioCare clinicians at target unit economics, waitlist-to-start under two weeks.

Phase 3 · AI-native provider

Scale

Own the whole journey: referral, matching and group composition, the parent curriculum, outcome measurement across cohorts, expansion to Texas and New York. The compounding asset is longitudinal, consented, per-child participation and outcome evidence — data nobody can buy and the basis for composing better groups than anyone else can.
