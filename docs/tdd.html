BrioCare · Technical Design Document

Reflexes, judgment, and a veto

How the BrioCare prototype works: deterministic reflexes wake a supervised model that judges whether to act at all, every word it wants to say passes the therapist's controls — and the microphone itself obeys the room: Brio never talks over a child, and a line that finds no air is given back, audited, unspoken. Every technology choice below names the alternatives weighed and the trade-off accepted; the behaviors were then hardened against live adversarial sessions with real users, and each fix is recorded in the appendix.

01 · The system at a glance

Triggers wake it, a menu bounds it, a human gates it

Two runtimes, sharply divided. A thin media worker (LiveKit Agents, Node) holds Brio's seat in the room: it transcribes every child's track separately, counts speaking time, and plays Brio's voice on its own published audio track — no decisions, but it owns the one thing only it can see: the live floor. The brain lives entirely in Convex: deterministic triggers watch the ledger and the clock, wake a single-call LLM actor with full context and a menu of legal actions, re-check whatever it returns, and put every voiced line through the therapist's controls. The division of judgment is deliberate: the brain decides; the floor decides delivery. A decided line is only a candidate until the room has space for it — the worker waits for a real gap, yields the line back if the room stays busy, and stops mid-sentence if a child starts talking. The actor may always answer do_nothing — and that answer is logged like any other. The session model is a breakout room: the therapist opens, presses the handoff, and from then on Brio leads the exercise while the therapist supervises — from inside the room or from the panel outside it — with the gate, the mute, and the named moves always in hand.

Colour is the legend for every diagram in this document: green is deterministic code, blue is a model call, grey is transport and state. Note what the pipeline does not contain — the web app and the worker never call each other. Both hold live subscriptions to the same tables, so every feature is “write a row, and every screen and the robot react.”

The consequential judgment — made once, then revised mid-design: rules recommend, the model decides. The first version of this architecture had a deterministic engine choosing every action, with the model only phrasing. We inverted it after facing what rules cannot do: they can count airtime, but they cannot read the room. A rule can say "Maya is quiet"; it cannot see that Maya went quiet because the last share was about something painful. Now deterministic triggers wake a supervised actor that sees full context and may always choose do_nothing — but the model still cannot act outside a menu deterministic code built, cannot exceed ceilings it doesn't control, and cannot reach the room past the therapist gate. Both rejected options are worth stating: pure rules were more testable (they survive as tripwires and rate-limiters), and an always-on speech-to-speech agent (OpenAI Realtime) would be more expressive (still rejected — a group facilitator is mostly silent, needs external governance and per-child attribution, and must be testable without audio; it returns when Brio should genuinely converse, Phase 2).

03 · Real-time flow

One invite, end to end

The walk below is the core loop the interview will trace, in the default fully-autonomous dial. The pace is a human facilitator's: a thought is allowed to finish, a decision follows within a couple of seconds, and the voice waits for a genuine gap in the room. Captions and the equity display are not actions; they stream continuously.

The default dial spends none of this on approval. On the 3-second dial the card waits out its veto window while the worker is already synthesizing, so the delay costs less than it reads; on suggest-only the card simply waits for a tap. Therapist-initiated moves skip the wait entirely. The two slow steps are deliberate: the 2.5 s debounce is what lets a slow child finish a sentence, and the floor gate is what keeps the answer off the top of their voice.

  1. The handoff. The therapist opens the session, then presses the green Hand to Brio button on Brio's own video tile. Until that introduce executes, the engine proposes nothing — Brio sits in the room silently, building the ledger. The introduction opens the exercise in the same breath and sets the norm once: passing is always okay.
  2. Sense a thought ending. Leo shares his favorite thing. STT fragments merge at write into one row per thought (same speaker, gaps under 2.5 s), and each landing fragment schedules a debounced wake check — only the check after the true end of the thought reaches the triggers, and only once the floor has been quiet ~2.5 s.
  3. Wake with a moment, not a name. Maya hasn't been heard since the handoff, so the round trigger fires — cooldowns, the 3-invite ceiling, weight, passes, and mute all checked deterministically first. For round and follow-up wakes the trigger deliberately pins no target: who goes next is transcript semantics, so the actor picks — from candidates the engine pre-filtered.
  4. Decide + write. One LLM call returns one structured action against a menu it cannot extend, with the utterance in the same call — or do_nothing, logged like any other. The commit mutation re-checks everything against live state atomically: menu legality, target eligibility, the pass rule, the ceiling, a mute that landed mid-flight, and a dedupe gap so two in-flight wakes can't both land. The card shows the exact words: "Maya, want to share yours? No pressure!"
  5. The dial, then the floor. On the default dial the intent executes instantly (a 3 s veto window on the middle dial; a required tap on suggest-only — and the therapist can mute or veto at any moment regardless). Executed means the decision is final — not that anyone heard it. The worker's delivery gate now waits for a real gap: no human speaking, ~2.5 s of silence. If the room stays busy for 12 s, it hands the line back — canceled (yielded), fully audited, never spoken — and the engine simply decides fresh later. If a child starts talking while Brio speaks, Brio stops mid-sentence: it always yields to children.
  6. Voice, stamped. Playback runs from pre-synthesized audio and the worker stamps voicedAt when it finishes — the record distinguishes what was decided from what children actually heard. Then the floor belongs to the kids: no new voiced move until someone responds (any share, reaction, or pass hands the floor back instantly) or 12 s of true silence runs out. Maya answers; her ledger moves; the round rolls on.

Therapist commands are Convex mutations — one path, transactional, replayable. Mute pre-empts at the audio layer too: a muted Brio cannot finish a sentence it already started rendering, because audio frames are gated at publish time, not at request time.

04 · Reflexes and judgment

Triggers wake; the actor decides; three dials gate

The triggers are pure TypeScript — no I/O, no model calls. State: the exercise description, a per-child ledger (airtime, action count, last contribution, prompts issued, passes, the therapist-set participation weight — zero removes a child from targeting entirely), and mode flags (muted, autonomy dial, whether the handoff has happened). A firing trigger wakes the actor with a recommendation, a reason, and a menu of legal actions — eligibility, cooldowns, and ceilings enforced before the model is ever called. Evaluation runs on two paths: a debounced check ~2.5 s after each utterance lands (the "someone just finished a thought" moment), and a 5 s tick as idle-detector and fallback. The whole pacing model is three concepts: the thought gap (let a thought finish, 2.5 s), turn-taking (≥12 s between decisions, plus a post-speech grace that ends the moment any child responds — a response is the floor handing back — or after 12 s of true silence), and the delivery gate (§05). Every wake logs event → trigger → decision, and the decision may be do_nothing.

The menu is the first veto. Each rung hands the actor a different list of legal actions, and an action that is not on that list is dead on arrival at commit. Notice what the round does not carry — a name. Which child the conversation actually points to is transcript semantics, so the actor chooses; the arithmetic of eligibility still binds it afterwards. Autonomous · default

Decisions execute instantly; the mute is the brake, and every line still shows on the panel as it happens. The demo runs here: Brio audibly leads at a human pace — the delivery gate, not an approval timer, sets the rhythm.

3-second delay · the middle dial

Each engine move waits a 3 s veto window before executing — one tap cancels, one tap approves early, and pre-synthesized audio makes execution at lapse instant. The countdown renders on the card.

Suggest-only · one tap away

Every card waits for the therapist's tap; nothing is voiced unbidden. Where a cautious clinician — or an early pilot — can choose to live. Cards whose moment passes cancel themselves as stale.

Cancellation is a state, not a deletion — which is what makes the review page and the actor’s own decision history possible. The two worker-driven edges are guarded identically: each requires a row that is still executed and not yet voiced, so a therapist veto racing playback can never be overwritten. A barge-in still stamps voicedAt, because partially heard is heard.

Intent lifecycle: pending → executed → voiced | canceled, with cancellationReason: therapist | muted | stale | yielded. Executed means the decision is final; voicedAt, stamped by the worker when playback finishes, means children actually heard it — and only executed moves (plus therapist vetoes) count as "targeting" a child for cooldown purposes: a yielded line the room never heard locks nobody out. Mute drops pending cards rather than queueing them; the triggers cancel their own cards when their premise dies. A pass is final: detected from the word ("pass", and "past" — its reliable STT garble), from a soft decline shortly after an invite ("No, I'm good"), or from a tapped Pass card, it makes the child untargetable for 3 minutes — enforced at the trigger and re-checked as a hard veto at commit, because prompt rules alone were observed failing. Speaking up again voluntarily clears it: opting back in is the child's call. do_nothing decisions are inserted directly as executed rows with the model's reason: no card, no display, full audit. Every actor row stores the prompt sent and the raw response received. Every threshold above is config, re-derived with the clinical partner in week one.

05 · Control & safety model

Four gates, in series

The PRD's boundary list is enforced structurally, not rhetorically. For Brio to say anything, four independent gates must pass — the human holds the third, and the room itself holds the last.

Gate 1 · the menu and the hard veto

The actor chooses from a menu deterministic code builds per wake-up: only legal actions, only eligible targets — weight-zero children, recent passes, over-ceiling and recently-invited kids never appear on it. do_nothing is always present, except on a therapist cue, where Brio must offer its best. The commit mutation then re-checks the decision against live tables — menu legality, target eligibility, the pass rule, the per-child ceiling, mid-flight mutes, and a dedupe gap against concurrent wakes. A violation lands as an audited do_nothing with the veto reason; nothing errors, nothing slips. Prompt rules were observed being ignored by the model; these were not.

Gate 2 · the crafted prompt

The same call that decides also writes, under a tightly specified system prompt: silence framed as the usually-correct answer, two sentences max, warm and low-pressure, easy outs on invitations (varied, never chanted — the norm is set once at the handoff), bridge-in-one-breath acknowledgments, a pass is final, no clinical language, no child-to-child comparisons. There is deliberately no post-generation model eval — the gates on either side cover what a judge model would, without its latency.

Gate 3 · the human

Three dials: instant (default — the mute is the brake), a 3 s veto window, or tap-to-approve. Every line shows on the panel with its reason either way; mute pre-empts at the audio-frame level and drops pending cards; a participation weight of zero removes a child from targeting entirely; cue and the named moves make Brio an instrument, not just an actor — and the therapist can supervise from outside the room entirely, panel in hand.

Gate 4 · the floor

The worker owns the microphone and never talks over a child. An executed line waits for a real gap (~2.5 s of live silence); if the room stays busy for 12 s the line is yielded — given back unspoken, audited as such, visible to the therapist and to the actor's own next decision. A child who starts talking while Brio speaks cuts it off mid-sentence within a second. Timing correctness lives here, at the only layer that can see the live floor — the brain never has to guess whether a slow speaker is finished.

This loop is why “Brio never talks over a child” is a mechanism rather than a prompt line — and it is the only place in the system that can see the live floor, which is why the delivery decision lives here and not in the brain. Verified live: a cue fired into a continuously talking room was held, then yielded — five yields and zero barge-ins across one adversarial session — and the next line, decided after the room quieted, waited its gap and landed clean.

The distress path runs two detectors. The tripwire: a deliberately dumb, conservative watchlist over every final transcript line — zero latency, zero cost, biased hard toward false positives. The reader: every 10 seconds, the safety evaluator — an actor with a two-item menu, raise_flag or do_nothing — reads the fresh transcript window for what a watchlist can never see: paraphrase, indirection, context. Both write to the same flags table, deduped. A hit changes nothing in any child's room — the exact line pins to a private, unmissable panel alert; no voiced response, no auto-escalation; the therapist owns every next step. Known limits are documented in the eval report; vocabulary, cadence, and the human protocol behind the flag are week-one work with the clinical partner.

Withheld powers, restated: Brio never controls a child's microphone (suggest-only), never moves children between rooms, never touches membership, never messages a child outside the logged, therapist-visible channel.

06 · The actor

How a decision gets made — and written, in the same breath

One LLM call per wake-up, three process shapes, one pattern. The facilitation actor receives the roster with weights and the therapist's pre-session notes, the entire session timeline — every transcribed thought, every reaction and selection answer (passes included), and Brio's own complete decision history: lines it spoke, moments it chose silence with their reasons, and moves that were vetoed, went stale, or were yielded, each marked "the children never heard it" — plus the trigger's recommendation and the menu. Self-knowledge is load-bearing: a yielded acknowledgment must fold into the opening of the next spoken line, an unanswered invite must not be repeated verbatim, and unsaid lines must never be referred to as said. It returns one structured action: {action, target?, utterance?, reason}, where the utterance is written in the same call, or do_nothing. The safety evaluator is the same pattern on a 10 s clock with a two-item menu. The one-off job runs at session end: a single call that writes every child's note in one go (the full session transcript is mechanical concatenation — no model involved). The prototype waits for completions; TTS pre-synthesizes the moment text exists. A failed call still lands as an audited do_nothing carrying the error; the trigger re-fires. No post-generation eval pass; the gates are the judge.

What the model receives, emits, and gets wrong — stated here because it is the question that matters: it receives (context, recommendation, menu); it emits one structured action carrying the sentence Brio will say — or do_nothing; and live sessions showed it going wrong in three characteristic ways. First, mis-transcribed child speech: the line confidently references something nobody said (mitigated by thought-level merging, and by the therapist seeing the exact words). Second, action bias: models would rather do something than stay silent — the prompt frames do_nothing as the expected answer, and menus shrink deterministically when the room says stop. Third — the empirical lesson of the live-testing cycle — prompt rules alone do not hold: the model named children in unnamed group cues, re-invited a child who said "I want to pass," and chanted its own example phrasing. Every one of those rules that matters is therefore also a deterministic mechanism: the name ban is a constraint veto, the pass is a commit-time hard stop, repetition is a menu drop. The prompt shapes behavior; the gates guarantee it.

07 · Session data

Six tables, and everything else derived

The append-only pair — utterances and actions — is the record: airtime reconstructs as Σ(endAt − startAt), the review timeline is a merge of four small queries, and notes read a child's own rows. An utterance row is a thought, not an STT fragment: a slow-speaking child's "And… sometimes… the…" merges at write into one honest row, which is what every consumer wants — the actor's context, the transcript, first-share detection, even the watchlist tripwire (which re-runs on the merged text and so catches phrases split across fragments). Hot state lives in the agent's memory; the counters on participants are its materialized view, and Convex live-syncs every screen. Three conventions replace fields: a participant's Convex _id doubles as their LiveKit identity, presence comes from LiveKit rather than the database, and Convex's built-in _creationTime stamps every insert.

Notes are generated in a single end-of-session call — one prompt receives every child's counters and their own lines, and returns all notes in one go, in the individualized-portion shape group documentation requires: objective counts first, quotes only from the child's own words, no clinical inference. They land on postSessionNote as drafts until the therapist edits and signs. Privacy posture, honestly: the demo runs synthetic and consenting-adult data only. A real deployment adds self-hosted LiveKit or BAA'd cloud, encrypted storage, minors' consent flows, and state-specific telehealth review — Phase 1 work, listed in the PRD's unvalidated assumptions.

08 · Deployment

Where everything runs

Four vendors, two of them already in hand (Render, Convex), and — at prototype scale — a $0 standing bill. Secrets never reach a browser: LiveKit room tokens are minted inside a Convex action, so clients only ever hold short-lived JWTs.

Everything meets in Convex, and only in Convex. The web app and the worker never call each other and share no process — both hold live subscriptions to the same tables, so a decision reaches the room simply by being written down: flipping an intent row to executed invalidates the worker’s subscription, which pushes the row, which queues the line. That one choice is what keeps the panel, the children’s screens and the robot’s voice in sync with no coordination code of ours.

09 · Evaluation

The demo cast is the test harness

Because the triggers are pure and the schema is shared, the same four scripted personas — the dominator, the silent one, the tangent-chaser, the eager helper — power the live demo (npm run demo — they join the room and speak through TTS, so Brio hears them with its real STT) and are designed to power a headless eval suite next (injecting events directly, no audio anywhere; the actor scripted for keyless invariant checks, live with an OpenAI key when judgment itself is being scored).

Unit · npm test

Fifty-eight keyless tests, in seconds: pure trigger reflexes (the handoff gate, the round, follow-up menus and their escalation guards, cooldowns, ceilings, grace-until-response), the hard constraints (including the observed level-0 name ban and the pass veto), guarded lifecycle races under fake timers (approve vs veto vs auto-execute vs mute vs dial flips vs yield), merge-at-write, pass detection incl. soft declines and opt-back-in, and the full trigger→actor→audited-commit loop through the no-key fallback path.

Scenarios · designed, next up

Scripted sessions replayed facilitation-on vs facilitation-off. Reported per run: participation Gini across actions and airtime, silent-persona time-to-first-action (target: < 4 min on; never, off), prompts-per-child ceiling respected, and the unnecessary-action rate — how often the actor speaks when annotated scenarios say do_nothing was right. Output: a written report.

Invariants · the property list

Property checks over every generated log: in suggest-only mode nothing executes without a therapist tap; nothing executes while muted; every spoken line traces to an intent row born with that exact text; canceled intents never reach audio; distress lines produce a flag and zero voiced responses; weight-zero children receive zero targeted intents; and every actor decision — do_nothing included — carries its prompt and raw response.

What this suite proves and what it cannot: it proves the mechanism — equity math, gating, boundaries — under scripted behavior. It cannot prove children behave like scripts, that clinicians trust the panel, or that STT holds up against real child speech in home acoustics. Those are exactly the PRD's week-one discovery items.

11 · With more time

What changes after 48 hours

First

  • Child-speech ASR evaluation — benchmark real child audio (consented corpora) across STT vendors; this is the riskiest technical assumption in the system
  • Clinical calibration — every threshold in the rule table re-derived with the therapist partner from replayed sessions
  • Distress detection beyond a watchlist — a classifier tuned with clinicians, with a measured false-negative story before any trust is placed in it

Then

  • Multi-room orchestration — v0 already IS the breakout room (Brio leads, the therapist supervises from outside); add the main room around it: several Brio-led rooms at once, one roving-therapist panel across them, Realtime speech-to-speech where Brio genuinely converses
  • Compliance infrastructure — self-hosted LiveKit SFU, BAA'd model providers, encrypted event log, consent and retention flows
  • The engagement layer — the pet token economy and group points, layered on once the participation loop is proven; a formal talking-stick mode for groups that need tighter structure
  • The between-sessions half — missions, parent loop, cohort memory (linking across weeks, therapist-triggered)

Appendix

How this design got here

The decisions that changed between the first sketch and this document, in order, each with the reason it moved — the last three driven by adversarial live sessions with real users. None of these were reversals for taste — each traded something away on purpose.

  1. Research first, features second. Field research into how pediatric group therapy actually runs (closed cohorts, the airtime arithmetic, the co-facilitator role split, the per-child documentation burden) set the thesis before any architecture existed: staff the second chair, don't invent a new clinical behavior.
  2. Scope collapsed to one structured exercise. The full 45-minute protocol — rituals, agreements, check-ins — became roadmap; v0 runs the single most facilitation-dense block end to end and exits into the record. No agenda machinery was built.
  3. The heads-up loop replaced act-then-apologize. Brio previews every planned line on the panel — the exact words — before the room hears it. What began as a courtesy became the third safety gate and reshaped the intent lifecycle around a veto.
  4. The talking stick was cut. Formal turn-taking proved too restrictive for a prototype; discussion stays free-flowing, the therapist moderates mics directly, and equity logic keys on airtime evidence instead of turn mechanics. The stick survives as a roadmap mode.
  5. Controls converged on fewer, stronger primitives. Pause died, then Hold died — Mute suffices once nothing is clock-driven. The quiet flag and the baseline multiplier merged into one live per-child participation weight, where zero means never targeted.
  6. The data model shrank from eight tables to six. Live state folded into sessions, counters and notes into participants; speaking events and transcripts merged into utterances (timing from speaking detection, text from STT, best-effort); ledger snapshots became materialized counters; derived values are computed, never stored; the pet economy was deferred whole.
  7. Speech went from guarded templates to generated lines. Validator chains and fallback races were removed as latency spent guarding against what the therapist preview already catches. The model writes Brio's actual words; TTS pre-synthesizes during the window so approval triggers playback, never generation.
  8. The big inversion: rules decide → rules recommend. Rules can count airtime but cannot read the room, so deterministic triggers now wake a full-context actor that may always choose do_nothing — bounded by a menu it cannot extend, hard constraints, and the human gate. Distress gained a 10-second LLM sweep beside the zero-latency tripwire, and every decision is audited with its prompt and raw response.
  9. The brain moved into Convex; the worker became a dumb pipe. All cognition lives in testable Convex functions; the agents-js process survives only because something must hold Brio's seat in the room — per-track STT in, a published voice track out.
  10. The stack settled on two keys and no standing bill. LiveKit (media + Inference STT + hosted agent) and OpenAI (gpt-5-mini + TTS), with the web on Render and the brain on Convex, at $0/month standing cost. The title followed the architecture: from "a deterministic brain with a narrow mouth" to "reflexes, judgment, and a veto."
  11. Co-facilitator became room-leader. The first live run exposed a role confusion: with the therapist and Brio in the same room, nobody knew who was leading. The deployment model was recast as the breakout room — therapist opens, presses the handoff on Brio's own tile, and may supervise from outside the room entirely. The engine gained the handoff gate (silent until introduced), the round (invite everyone once — the actor picks who's next from the transcript, because "who has truly shared" is semantics, not arithmetic), and the follow-up phase; the gentle L0/L1 ladder became the fallback path. The autonomy dial split into three, with instant execution the new default: at leading pace, the mute is a better brake than a timer.
  12. The floor took over timing — and the patches came out. Real users exposed what row-timestamps can't see: STT fragments a slow child's thought (fixed by merge-at-write: one row per thought), and no text heuristic can know a speaker is finished (a trailing-words detector was built, beaten by the very next test, and deleted whole). The systematic fix split deciding from delivering: executed → voiced with a delivery gate in the worker — wait for a live gap, yield the line back if the room stays busy, stop mid-sentence when a child speaks. Pacing collapsed to three narratable concepts (thought gap, turn-taking with grace-until-response, the floor), and two whole timer constants were deleted rather than tuned.
  13. Prompt rules were promoted to mechanisms, one observed failure at a time. Live sessions proved the model ignores instructions under pressure: it re-invited a child five times — twice after "I want to pass"; it chanted its own example phrasing; it fired four group activities into 75 seconds of silence. Each failure became deterministic: pass-is-final as a commit-time veto (with soft declines — "No, I'm good" — detected in context, and a voluntary share clearing the pass), the invite ceiling enforced at commit across every trigger path, unanswered prompts shrinking the menu, and the actor's context expanded to its own full decision history — including yielded lines marked "the children never heard it" — so it can bridge, vary, and never cite words that were never said. The doctrine the cycle produced: the prompt shapes behavior; the gates guarantee it.
