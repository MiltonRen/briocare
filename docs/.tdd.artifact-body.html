BrioCare · Technical Design Document

Reflexes, judgment, and a veto

How the BrioCare prototype works: deterministic reflexes wake a supervised model that judges whether to act at all — and every word it wants to say passes the therapist first. Every technology choice below names the alternatives weighed and the trade-off accepted.

v0.7 · 29 August 2026 Author · Milton Ren Status · build-challenge submission Companions · PRD + field research

01 · The system at a glance

Triggers wake it, a menu bounds it, a human gates it

Two runtimes, sharply divided. A thin media worker (LiveKit Agents, Node) holds Brio's seat in the room: it transcribes every child's track separately, counts speaking time, and plays Brio's voice on its own published audio track — no decisions. The brain lives entirely in Convex: deterministic triggers watch the ledger and the clock, wake a single-call LLM actor with full context and a menu of legal actions, re-check whatever it returns, and put every voiced line through the therapist gate. The actor may always answer do_nothing — and that answer is logged like any other.

The consequential judgment — made once, then revised mid-design: rules recommend, the model decides. The first version of this architecture had a deterministic engine choosing every action, with the model only phrasing. We inverted it after facing what rules cannot do: they can count airtime, but they cannot read the room. A rule can say "Maya is quiet"; it cannot see that Maya went quiet because the last share was about something painful. Now deterministic triggers wake a supervised actor that sees full context and may always choose do_nothing — but the model still cannot act outside a menu deterministic code built, cannot exceed ceilings it doesn't control, and cannot reach the room past the therapist gate. Both rejected options are worth stating: pure rules were more testable (they survive as tripwires and rate-limiters), and an always-on speech-to-speech agent (OpenAI Realtime) would be more expressive (still rejected — a group facilitator is mostly silent, needs external governance and per-child attribution, and must be testable without audio; it returns for breakout rooms, Phase 2).

Design rule that makes it safe: the sub-second hot path lives in agent memory; Convex receives counter updates and completed rows, never per-audio-frame writes.
Frontend React + Vite + TS@livekit/components-react vs Next.js — no SSR, no routes worth server-rendering; Vite is faster to build and simpler to run. React is the author's home ground, and LiveKit's React components cover room plumbing so build time goes to the panel, not to WebRTC glue.
Phrasing + notes OpenAI gpt-5-mini (text)the actor, the safety evaluator, the notes writer — one key with TTS Any capable text model works — the design does not depend on model choice. The actor receives full context plus a menu of legal actions and returns one structured action — or do_nothing — with the utterance text in the same call; deterministic code re-checks the output, and the therapist still previews every word. Deliberately no post-generation eval pass (the human gate covers it, without the latency), and the prototype waits for completions rather than racing fallbacks. The full prompt and raw response are stored on every intent row for audit. A small OpenAI model keeps the stack on two keys: LiveKit + OpenAI.

03 · Real-time flow

One draw-out, end to end

The walk below is the core loop the interview will trace, in the default autonomous mode. Latency targets are stated per hop; the ~4 s veto window dominates — consent, not lag (suggest-only mode swaps the window for the therapist's own tap). Captions and the equity display are not actions; they stream continuously.

  1. Sense. Maya has 0:12 of speech and one reaction in 14 minutes; her share is far below her participation weight. The trigger is audio-level accounting — it works even if STT is degraded.
  2. Wake. The draw-out trigger fires at the lowest untried ladder level — cooldowns, ceiling, weight, and mute all checked deterministically (a weight of zero would have stopped the wake here). It assembles the actor's input: full context, the recommendation draw_out(maya, level_0) with its reason, and the menu of legal actions.
  3. Decide + write. One LLM call returns one structured action. The actor may follow the recommendation, choose a different legal action, or return do_nothing — which is logged to agentIntents with its reason, no card shown. Here it agrees and writes the line; the row is inserted with its text final, plus the full prompt and raw response for audit. TTS synthesis kicks off immediately in parallel, so the audio exists before anyone approves anything. The card shows the exact words: "Let's hear from some voices we haven't heard yet — thumbs up or down: was this a busy week?"
  4. Window. A ~4 s veto window runs while the card sits on the panel. One tap cancels; one tap approves early; untouched, the window lapses and auto-approves. Every transition is a guarded Convex mutation that succeeds only while the intent is still pending — approve and cancel race atomically, and the database decides. If the room had moved on first, the engine would already have canceled the card as stale.
  5. Speak. Playback starts the instant the transition lands — the audio was synthesized while the window ran. Brio's track plays the line; captions mirror it; the row is marked executed.
  6. Close the loop. Maya sends a thumbs-up reaction. The action lands in her ledger, her deficit shrinks, and the level-1 escalation that would have followed never fires. Approval-to-audio is near-instant; the end-to-end pace belongs to the veto window, by design.

Therapist commands are Convex mutations — one path, transactional, replayable. Mute pre-empts at the audio layer too: a muted Brio cannot finish a sentence it already started rendering, because audio frames are gated at publish time, not at request time.

04 · Reflexes and judgment

Triggers wake; the actor decides; two autonomy modes gate

The triggers are pure TypeScript — no I/O, no model calls. State: the exercise description, a per-child ledger (airtime, action count, last contribution, prompts issued, the therapist-set participation weight — zero removes a child from targeting entirely), and mode flags (muted, autonomy dial). They no longer decide anything: a firing trigger wakes the actor with a recommendation, a reason, and a menu of legal actions — cooldowns and ceilings enforced before the model is ever called, which also bounds LLM cost and cadence. Every wake logs event → trigger → decision, and the decision may be do_nothing.

Autonomous · default

Voiced lines auto-execute after a ~4 s veto window unless canceled — and because the audio is pre-synthesized, execution at lapse is instant. The demo runs here: Brio visibly co-facilitates.

Suggest-only · one tap away

Every card waits for the therapist's tap; nothing is voiced unbidden. Where a cautious clinician — or an early pilot — can choose to live. Cue-initiated speech executes on the tap in either mode: the tap is consent.

Always-on displays

Captions, the equity view, and flags are not intents at all — they stream continuously and are unaffected by the dial or by mute.

Intent lifecycle: pending → executed | canceled, with cancellationReason: therapist | muted | stale. Mute drops pending cards rather than queueing them, and the triggers cancel their own cards when their premise dies — the quiet child starts talking, or the moment passes. do_nothing decisions are inserted directly as executed rows with the model's reason: no card, no display, full audit. Every actor row stores the prompt sent and the raw response received. Voiced rows are born with their final text; announce time is the row's creation time. Every threshold above is config, re-derived with the clinical partner in week one.

05 · Control & safety model

Three gates, in series

The PRD's boundary list is enforced structurally, not rhetorically. For Brio to say anything, three independent gates must pass — and the therapist sits in front of the last one.

Gate 1 · the menu

The actor chooses from a menu deterministic code builds per wake-up: only legal actions, only eligible targets — weight-zero and over-ceiling children never appear on it. do_nothing is always present, except on a therapist cue, where Brio must offer its best. Diagnosis, advice, and free chat aren't low-probability outputs; they're not on the menu, and the output is re-checked against it.

Gate 2 · the crafted prompt

The same call that decides also writes, under a tightly specified system prompt: silence is framed as the usually-correct answer, two sentences max, warm and low-pressure, invitations always carry a pass, no clinical language, no child-to-child comparisons, group-addressed content by default. Zero-cost string sanity checks run inline; there is deliberately no post-generation model eval — the human gate covers what a judge model would, without its latency.

Gate 3 · the human

Every line sits on the panel before it plays: autonomous mode (the default) gives a veto window; suggest-only requires the tap. Mute pre-empts at the audio-frame level; a participation weight of zero removes a child from targeting entirely; cue and the named moves make Brio an instrument, not just an actor.

The distress path runs two detectors. The tripwire: a deliberately dumb, conservative watchlist over every final transcript line — zero latency, zero cost, biased hard toward false positives. The reader: every 10 seconds, the safety evaluator — an actor with a two-item menu, raise_flag or do_nothing — reads the fresh transcript window for what a watchlist can never see: paraphrase, indirection, context. Both write to the same flags table, deduped. A hit changes nothing in any child's room — the exact line pins to a private, unmissable panel alert; no voiced response, no auto-escalation; the therapist owns every next step. Known limits are documented in the eval report; vocabulary, cadence, and the human protocol behind the flag are week-one work with the clinical partner.

Withheld powers, restated: Brio never controls a child's microphone (suggest-only), never moves children between rooms, never touches membership, never messages a child outside the logged, therapist-visible channel.

06 · The actor

How a decision gets made — and written, in the same breath

One LLM call per wake-up, three process shapes, one pattern. The facilitation actor receives the roster with weights, a ledger summary, the last ~90 s of transcript, Brio's own recent actions, the exercise description, and the trigger's recommendation with its reason — plus the menu. It returns one structured action: {action, target?, utterance?, reason}, where the utterance is written in the same call, or do_nothing. The safety evaluator is the same pattern on a 10 s clock with a two-item menu. The one-off job runs at session end: a single call that writes every child's note in one go (the full session transcript is mechanical concatenation — no model involved). Session start needs no special process — the therapist speaks first and cues Brio's introduction like any other cue. The prototype waits for completions; TTS starts the moment text exists, so approval triggers playback, never generation. A failed call means no card this wake; the trigger re-fires. No post-generation eval pass; the therapist preview is the judge.

What the model receives, emits, and gets wrong — stated here because it is the question that matters: it receives (context, recommendation, menu); it emits one structured action carrying the sentence Brio will say — or do_nothing; and it goes wrong in two characteristic ways. First, mis-transcribed child speech: the line confidently references something nobody said (mitigated by STT-confidence gating on the window, and by the therapist reading the exact words first). Second, action bias: language models would rather do something than stay silent, and a facilitator's best move is usually silence — so the prompt frames do_nothing as the expected answer, and the unnecessary-action rate is measured explicitly in the scenario evals. Both failures are reproduced deliberately in the eval fixtures.

07 · Session data

Six tables, and everything else derived

The append-only pair — utterances and actions — is the record: airtime reconstructs as Σ(endAt − startAt), the review timeline is a merge of four small queries, and notes read a child's own rows. Hot state lives in the agent's memory; the counters on participants are its materialized view, and Convex live-syncs every screen. Three conventions replace fields: a participant's Convex _id doubles as their LiveKit identity, presence comes from LiveKit rather than the database, and Convex's built-in _creationTime stamps every insert.

Notes are generated in a single end-of-session call — one prompt receives every child's counters and their own lines, and returns all notes in one go, in the individualized-portion shape group documentation requires: objective counts first, quotes only from the child's own words, no clinical inference. They land on postSessionNote as drafts until the therapist edits and signs. Privacy posture, honestly: the demo runs synthetic and consenting-adult data only. A real deployment adds self-hosted LiveKit or BAA'd cloud, encrypted storage, minors' consent flows, and state-specific telehealth review — Phase 1 work, listed in the PRD's unvalidated assumptions.

08 · Deployment

Where everything runs

Four vendors, two of them already in hand (Render, Convex), and — at prototype scale — a $0 standing bill. Secrets never reach a browser: LiveKit room tokens are minted inside a Convex action, so clients only ever hold short-lived JWTs.

09 · Evaluation

The demo cast is the test harness

Because the triggers are pure and the schema is shared, the same four scripted personas — the dominator, the silent one, the tangent-chaser, the eager helper — power the live demo (joining the room as real participants) and the headless eval suite (injecting events directly, no audio anywhere; the actor runs scripted for keyless invariant checks, and live with an OpenAI key when judgment itself is being scored).

Unit · npm test

Trigger reflexes in isolation: thresholds, cooldowns, ladder order, menu construction (who is eligible, what is legal), lifecycle transitions, prompt-context assembly (what the actor is and isn't shown), and the sanity checks. No network, no keys, seconds to run.

Scenarios · npm run eval

Scripted sessions replayed facilitation-on vs facilitation-off. Reported per run: participation Gini across actions and airtime, silent-persona time-to-first-action (target: < 4 min on; never, off), prompts-per-child ceiling respected, and the unnecessary-action rate — how often the actor speaks when annotated scenarios say do_nothing was right. Output is a written report the submission links.

Invariants · in both

Property checks over every generated log: in suggest-only mode nothing executes without a therapist tap; nothing executes while muted; every spoken line traces to an intent row born with that exact text; canceled intents never reach audio; distress lines produce a flag and zero voiced responses; weight-zero children receive zero targeted intents; and every actor decision — do_nothing included — carries its prompt and raw response.

What the suite proves and what it cannot: it proves the mechanism — equity math, gating, boundaries — under scripted behavior. It cannot prove children behave like scripts, that clinicians trust the panel, or that STT holds up against real child speech in home acoustics. Those are exactly the PRD's week-one discovery items; the eval report says so on its first page.

11 · With more time

What changes after 48 hours

First

  • Child-speech ASR evaluation — benchmark real child audio (consented corpora) across STT vendors; this is the riskiest technical assumption in the system
  • Clinical calibration — every threshold in the rule table re-derived with the therapist partner from replayed sessions
  • Distress detection beyond a watchlist — a classifier tuned with clinicians, with a measured false-negative story before any trust is placed in it

Then

  • Breakout rooms — sessions already model rooms; add room-scoped engine instances and a roving-therapist panel; adopt Realtime speech-to-speech inside breakouts where Brio genuinely converses
  • Compliance infrastructure — self-hosted LiveKit SFU, BAA'd model providers, encrypted event log, consent and retention flows
  • The engagement layer — the pet token economy and group points, layered on once the participation loop is proven; a formal talking-stick mode for groups that need tighter structure
  • The between-sessions half — missions, parent loop, cohort memory (linking across weeks, therapist-triggered)

Appendix

How this design got here

The decisions that changed between the first sketch and this document, in order, each with the reason it moved. None of these were reversals for taste — each traded something away on purpose.

  1. Research first, features second. Field research into how pediatric group therapy actually runs (closed cohorts, the airtime arithmetic, the co-facilitator role split, the per-child documentation burden) set the thesis before any architecture existed: staff the second chair, don't invent a new clinical behavior.
  2. Scope collapsed to one structured exercise. The full 45-minute protocol — rituals, agreements, check-ins — became roadmap; v0 runs the single most facilitation-dense block end to end and exits into the record. No agenda machinery was built.
  3. The heads-up loop replaced act-then-apologize. Brio previews every planned line on the panel — the exact words — before the room hears it. What began as a courtesy became the third safety gate and reshaped the intent lifecycle around a veto.
  4. The talking stick was cut. Formal turn-taking proved too restrictive for a prototype; discussion stays free-flowing, the therapist moderates mics directly, and equity logic keys on airtime evidence instead of turn mechanics. The stick survives as a roadmap mode.
  5. Controls converged on fewer, stronger primitives. Pause died, then Hold died — Mute suffices once nothing is clock-driven. The quiet flag and the baseline multiplier merged into one live per-child participation weight, where zero means never targeted.
  6. The data model shrank from eight tables to six. Live state folded into sessions, counters and notes into participants; speaking events and transcripts merged into utterances (timing from speaking detection, text from STT, best-effort); ledger snapshots became materialized counters; derived values are computed, never stored; the pet economy was deferred whole.
  7. Speech went from guarded templates to generated lines. Validator chains and fallback races were removed as latency spent guarding against what the therapist preview already catches. The model writes Brio's actual words; TTS pre-synthesizes during the window so approval triggers playback, never generation.
  8. The big inversion: rules decide → rules recommend. Rules can count airtime but cannot read the room, so deterministic triggers now wake a full-context actor that may always choose do_nothing — bounded by a menu it cannot extend, hard constraints, and the human gate. Distress gained a 10-second LLM sweep beside the zero-latency tripwire, and every decision is audited with its prompt and raw response.
  9. The brain moved into Convex; the worker became a dumb pipe. All cognition lives in testable Convex functions; the agents-js process survives only because something must hold Brio's seat in the room — per-track STT in, a published voice track out.
  10. The stack settled on two keys and no standing bill. LiveKit (media + Inference STT + hosted agent) and OpenAI (gpt-5-mini + TTS), with the web on Render and the brain on Convex, at $0/month standing cost. Autonomous mode became the default — instant execution at window lapse makes Brio visibly co-facilitate — with suggest-only one tap away. The title followed the architecture: from "a deterministic brain with a narrow mouth" to "reflexes, judgment, and a veto."
